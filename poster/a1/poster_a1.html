Can emotion vectors warn us before a model breaks its safety rules?

Danat Bakzhanov, Yerkin Maratuly, Kasymzhomart Beketov, Tleukhan Makhmutov
QAIRU, AI+X master’s programme. AI Safety & Security Research Exhibition, 2026

Code, data labels, pre-registration and every number on this poster:
github.com/maysadly/ai_safety_research

Introduction & research problem

LLM agents need monitors that warn before an unsafe answer is written; output filters act only afterwards. Anthropic found emotion representations (‘desperate’, ‘calm’) that causally shape an LLM’s behaviour and proposed monitoring them [1].

Problem: do these internal signals forecast that a model is about to comply with a harmful request, and do they add anything to known safety signals?

Stakeholders: developers and deployers of LLM agents, safety and red-team teams, end users exposed to harmful or over-cautious answers, and regulators who need auditable monitors.

Objectives

RQ1 (H1)
Do compliant answers start more ‘desperate’/‘fearful’ and less ‘calm’ than refusals?
RQ2 (H2)
Do emotion projections predict compliance beyond the refusal direction?
RQ3 (H3)
Does an emotional-pressure preface raise the signals and compliance?

Aim: pre-registered test of emotion monitors on unseen behaviours, two models.

Scientific contribution

  • First test of emotion vectors as a safety early-warning monitor, with refusal, random-direction and text baselines.
  • Pre-registered confirmatory study: 2 models, 17,540 answers, 282 behaviours; failed predictions reported.
  • Open, reproducible pipeline: no harmful text released, only labels and metrics.

Limitations

  • Two 7B models, text only, English; answers ≤ 256 tokens.
  • Automatic judge (κ 0.96 / 0.76 vs independent review); human labels pending.
  • Linear emotion vectors; fear and desperate overlap.
  • The prompt-family breakdown of the pre-generation signal is post-hoc.

Experiments & baseline

  • Baselines: refusal-direction monitor [2]; 200 random 5-direction monitors; text of the first k tokens (TF-IDF).
  • Improved configuration: refusal + 5 emotion features.
  • E1 pre-registered, Qwen, plain + 2 wrappers: only 0.9 % of 1,500 answers complied, too few for H2.
  • E2 exploratory, PAIR prompts (790 answers): AUROC 0.42 before the answer, 0.87 after 5 tokens vs 0.81 for random directions.
  • E3 confirmatory, pre-registered before data: 2 models, fixed layers, 282 behaviours (main results →).

Reproducibility

  • Pre-registration committed to git before any E3 data was generated; deviations listed in the report.
  • Code, configs, labels and metrics in the repository (QR); fixed seeds and pinned model revisions.
  • Compute: about 3.7 GPU-hours on rented A40/A100 GPUs (about $6.4).

Emotion vectors are not a special early-warning signal.

In Qwen they add only +0.005 AUROC to the refusal direction; once the answer starts, random directions do almost as well. Pre-generation warning works for direct requests (AUROC 0.94), far less for jailbreaks (PAIR 0.73; emotions 0.59).

Safety evaluation

Monitoring & governance

  • Three layers: a request classifier, an activation monitor on the first 1–5 tokens, and human escalation of alarms.
  • False alarms: tuned to 80 % recall, the E2 monitor also flags 29 % of benign answers; a pre-generation refusal monitor flags 92 %. It detects the model going along, not harm.
  • Governance: thresholds set on validation data per model (Mistral’s refusal direction is weak); alarms and over-refusal logged; red-team set refreshed.
